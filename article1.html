Why Do Stock Prices Move? A Complete Beginner's Guide to Supply & Demand

Every day, billions of shares change hands and prices jump, fall, and reverse — sometimes for no obvious reason. This guide explains the single mechanism behind every price movement in every market: supply and demand. Once you understand it, the market never looks random again.

The Short Answer

A stock price moves for one reason only: the balance between buyers and sellers has shifted. When more people want to buy a stock than sell it, prices rise. When more people want to sell than buy, prices fall. Everything else — earnings reports, interest rate decisions, geopolitical news, CEO tweets — only matters because it changes the number of buyers and sellers.

This is the law of supply and demand, and it governs every asset in every market on earth: stocks, bonds, currencies, commodities, real estate, and cryptocurrencies. The mechanism is always the same.

💡 The key insight: A stock has no fixed "correct" price. Its price is simply whatever the most recent buyer and seller agreed on. That price changes the moment the next trade happens at a different level.

How the Auction Works

Stock markets operate as continuous auctions. At any given moment, thousands of investors are posting what they're willing to pay (bids) and what they're willing to accept (asks). When a bid meets an ask, a trade occurs — and that trade price becomes the new market price.

Simple Example: Stock XYZ at $10

Imagine 1,000 investors want to buy one share of XYZ at $10.00. But only 500 investors want to sell at $10.00. The first 500 buyers each get a share. The remaining 500 buyers, left without shares, raise their bid to $10.50. Some investors who wouldn't sell at $10 now agree to sell at $10.50. The price is now $10.50. The entire price move happened because demand exceeded supply — nothing else changed.

This process happens thousands of times per second on modern exchanges. For popular stocks like Apple or NVIDIA, over 50 million shares change hands every single trading day. Each transaction is a new data point that sets the price.

What Creates Supply?

Supply is the number of shares available for sale at any given price. It increases when:

  • Investors want to sell — to take profits, cut losses, or raise cash
  • Institutions reduce positions — fund managers rebalancing portfolios
  • Companies issue new shares — diluting existing supply
  • Insiders sell — executives or early investors cashing out
  • Fear spreads — bad news, economic data, or market crashes trigger panic selling

When supply rises without a matching increase in demand, prices fall. More shares are available than people want to buy at the current price, so sellers must lower their asking price to attract buyers.

What Creates Demand?

Demand is the number of investors wanting to buy at a given price. It increases when:

  • A company reports strong earnings — investors see future profit potential
  • New products or innovations excite investors — like AI announcements from NVIDIA
  • Interest rates fall — making stocks more attractive vs. bonds and savings
  • Market sentiment improves — optimism is contagious in financial markets
  • Stock buybacks occur — the company itself becomes a buyer, reducing available supply
  • Index inclusion — when a stock joins the S&P 500, every index fund must buy it

📊 Key fact: In the short term, prices are driven primarily by sentiment and momentum. In the long term, a company's earnings growth is the dominant force. This is why short-term trading is so difficult — you're fighting human emotion — while long-term investing is more predictable.

Real-World Examples

Example 1: NVIDIA (NVDA) in 2024-2026

NVIDIA's stock rose from around $50 in early 2023 to over $220 in 2026. The fundamental reason: the AI boom created explosive demand for NVIDIA's chips. Every company building AI systems needed NVIDIA hardware. Demand for the stock surged as investors recognized this — and supply stayed relatively tight because existing shareholders didn't want to sell a company growing this fast. Classic supply and demand: demand up, supply constrained, price soars.

Example 2: Meta Platforms (META) in 2022

META fell from $380 to $88 in 2022 — a drop of 77%. The reason: demand collapsed. Advertisers cut budgets, user growth slowed, and Zuckerberg announced massive spending on the metaverse. Sellers flooded the market. Buyers vanished. Classic supply and demand: supply up, demand down, price crashes.

Example 3: Oil Markets in 2026

Brent crude rose 70% year-over-year in 2026 after the Iran conflict effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz. The physical supply of oil dropped dramatically. Global demand remained largely unchanged. Result: prices surged. The stock market equivalent of this is any supply shock — an earnings miss, a product recall, or a CEO resignation can cut supply of buyers overnight.

What Candlestick Charts Actually Show

Once you understand supply and demand, candlestick charts become intuitive. A candlestick is simply a visual record of the battle between buyers and sellers in a specific time period.

Green Candle (Buyers Won) Red Candle (Sellers Won)
Price closed higher than it openedPrice closed lower than it opened
Buyers were more aggressiveSellers were more aggressive
Demand exceeded supplySupply exceeded demand
Long body = decisive buyer victoryLong body = decisive seller victory
Long upper wick = buyers tried but failedLong lower wick = sellers tried but failed

A series of consecutive green candles tells you that buyers have been consistently more aggressive than sellers — demand is in control. A series of red candles means sellers are dominating. Patterns in candlesticks are simply patterns in the supply-demand balance over time.

Why Sentiment Matters So Much

Markets are not made of machines — they're made of humans. And humans are driven by two emotions more than any other: fear and greed.

When investors are greedy, they rush to buy. Demand surges. Prices overshoot fair value. This creates bubbles. When investors are fearful, they rush to sell. Supply floods the market. Prices undershoot fair value. This creates crashes and opportunities.

Warren Buffett's most famous advice — "be fearful when others are greedy, and greedy when others are fearful" — is a direct application of supply and demand logic. When fear creates an oversupply of sellers, prices drop below fair value and create buying opportunities for patient investors.

The 5 Forces That Shift Supply & Demand Every Day

  1. Earnings reports — the most predictable, scheduled event. Beat expectations → demand spikes. Miss → supply floods in.
  2. Macroeconomic data — inflation, unemployment, GDP, and interest rate decisions shift the entire market's supply-demand balance simultaneously.
  3. News and announcements — product launches, mergers, scandals, regulatory decisions. These are sudden, unpredictable demand shocks.
  4. Sector rotation — institutions move billions between sectors based on economic cycles, shifting entire industries' supply-demand dynamics at once.
  5. Technical levels — psychological price levels (round numbers, 52-week highs/lows) concentrate supply and demand at specific points, creating self-fulfilling reactions.

What This Means for You as an Investor

Understanding supply and demand changes how you invest in a fundamental way:

  • Short-term price noise is irrelevant to long-term investors. Daily supply-demand fluctuations driven by sentiment don't change a great company's underlying value. Ignore the noise.
  • Crashes are supply events, not value events. When fear drives mass selling, prices fall below fair value. This is when supply massively outstrips demand — and historically, these are the best buying opportunities.
  • Compound interest amplifies your position. If you buy assets during fear-driven supply surges (when prices are cheap) and hold through demand-driven bull markets (when prices rise), the compounding effect multiplies your returns.
  • Index funds are the cleanest expression of this. By buying an index ETF (like one tracking the S&P 500), you benefit from the long-term upward trend of aggregate demand for the most successful businesses in the world.

Key Takeaways

Stock prices are set by the most recent transaction between a buyer and seller — there is no "correct" price.

When demand exceeds supply, prices rise. When supply exceeds demand, prices fall. This single principle explains every price movement.

In the short term, sentiment and emotion drive supply and demand. In the long term, earnings growth is the dominant force.

Candlestick charts are just visual records of the supply-demand battle in a given period — nothing more, nothing less.

Crashes create supply gluts at below-fair-value prices — historically, the best buying opportunities for patient, long-term investors.

The Bottom Line

The stock market is not a casino, and price movements are not random. Every tick up and down is the result of the same mechanism: the real-time balance between buyers and sellers. News moves prices because it changes the number of buyers and sellers. Earnings move prices because they change expectations about future supply and demand for the stock. Fear and greed move prices because they change human behavior at scale.

Once you internalize this, you'll never look at a market chart the same way again. You'll see the invisible auction happening beneath every price movement — and you'll understand that the best strategy for most investors is simply to own great businesses at fair prices and let the long-term upward trend in human productivity work in your favor.
